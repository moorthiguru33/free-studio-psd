39	What is WebP?
40	WebP is an image format developed by Google and released in 2010. It supports both lossy compression (like JPG) and lossless compression (like PNG), plus transparency and animation (like GIF) – all in one format. At the same visual quality, lossy WebP files are typically 25–35% smaller than JPG, and lossless WebP files are around 25% smaller than PNG. That is why websites, online shops and content delivery networks serve images as WebP whenever the browser supports it, and every major browser has supported it since 2020.
41	The downside is software outside the browser. Older versions of Windows Photo Viewer, Microsoft Office, Photoshop before version 23.2, many print services and plenty of upload forms do not accept WebP. Converting to JPG is the universal fix.
42	Should you keep WebP or convert?
43	If a file is only going to be viewed in a browser or posted back to the web, there is no need to convert – WebP is smaller and every modern browser and most social networks accept it. Convert to JPG when the destination is software or a service that does not support WebP: older Office and photo editors, print shops, some e-mail clients, government and exam upload forms, and many marketplaces' bulk-upload tools. If you are unsure, JPG is the format that works absolutely everywhere, at the cost of a slightly larger file. Keep the original WebP too if you might need transparency or the smaller size again later; converting JPG back to WebP will not restore any detail that was lost.

75	Why images save as WebP
76	When you save an image from a website, you get whatever format the site sent. Most big sites detect that your browser supports WebP and send WebP to save bandwidth, even if the original upload was a JPG. Some browsers let you choose: in Chrome you can sometimes open the image in a new tab and change the file extension in the URL, but that only works on sites that serve different formats per URL. Converting the saved file is simpler and always works.
