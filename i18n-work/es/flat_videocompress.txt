48	Target size: how it works
49	A video's file size is simply its bitrate multiplied by its duration. If you ask for 25 MB and the clip is 2 minutes long, the compressor has 25 × 8 = 200 megabits to spend over 120 seconds – about 1.6 megabits per second. It reserves a small amount for audio (64–96 kbps) and container overhead, and gives the rest to the video encoder. Short clips can therefore look excellent even at small targets, while long videos need either a larger target or a lower resolution to look good.

85	Quality, bitrate and resolution
86	Three things determine how good a compressed video looks: the **bitrate** (how many bits per second the encoder may use), the **resolution** (how many pixels per frame) and the **codec** (how cleverly those bits are used). Lowering the resolution from 1080p to 720p reduces the number of pixels by more than half, so at the same bitrate each pixel gets twice as many bits – which is why a 720p video often looks better than a 1080p one at small sizes, especially on phones. Busy scenes – sports, confetti, water, leaves in wind – need more bitrate than static talking heads or screen recordings.
87	Codecs: H.264 vs VP9
88	MP4 output uses **H.264 (AVC)**, the most compatible video codec: it plays on every phone, computer, TV, browser and social network. WebM output uses **VP9**, which typically produces 20–40% smaller files at the same quality but is less universally supported in older apps and devices. MOV and MKV containers are offered for workflows that require them. For sharing, MP4/H.264 is the safe choice.

136	Glossary

165	Resolution: when to downscale
166	Phones show video on screens around 6 inches wide, where 720p already looks sharp; laptops benefit from 1080p, and only large TVs and monitors really need 4K. For sharing, downscaling from 4K to 1080p cuts the pixel count by three quarters, and from 1080p to 720p by more than half – the single most effective way to reduce size while keeping the picture clean. Downscaling also gives the encoder more bits per pixel, so fine detail and motion look better than in a full-resolution file squeezed to the same size. Keep the original resolution for screen recordings with small text, or for footage you will edit later; for everything else, 720p or 1080p is the practical choice.

Because processing is local, retrying costs only a few seconds or minutes, not another upload.
