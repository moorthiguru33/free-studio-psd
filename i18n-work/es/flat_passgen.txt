43	How strong is strong enough?
44	Password strength is measured in bits of **entropy** – the number of guesses an attacker would need, expressed as a power of two. Each extra bit doubles the work. A random password's entropy is its length × log₂(size of the character set). With all 94 printable characters, each character adds about 6.55 bits; with digits only, each adds 3.32 bits. Each word chosen at random from the 7,776-word EFF list adds 12.9 bits.
45	The table below assumes an attacker who has stolen a database of password hashes and can try 10 billion guesses per second – realistic for a fast, unsalted hash on modern graphics cards. Slow hashes such as bcrypt or Argon2 cut that rate by a factor of thousands or millions, and online attacks against a login page are slower still. The times are averages (half the search space).

99	Passwords versus passphrases

122	Why your passwords must be unique
123	The biggest danger is not that an attacker will guess one strong password; it is that a password you reused is leaked by one website and then tried everywhere else – a technique called **credential stuffing**. Billions of leaked username and password pairs circulate online. If your shopping-site password is the same as your e-mail password, a breach of the shop hands over your e-mail – and with it, password resets for everything else. Generate a different password for every account and let a password manager or your browser store them.
124	Patterns that make passwords weak
125	**Dictionary words with small changes** – Summer2024!, P@ssw0rd, Mumbai123. Cracking tools try every word with common substitutions, capitals and years first.
126	**Personal information** – names, birthdays, pet names, phone numbers, vehicle numbers and cricket teams are easy to find on social media.
127	**Keyboard patterns and sequences** – qwerty, 1q2w3e4r, asdfgh, 123456, abcd.
128	**Short length** – an 8-character password, even with symbols, can be brute-forced in hours by a determined attacker with a stolen hash.
129	**Reuse with a counter** – Password1, Password2, Password3 when forced to change.
130	**Human-chosen 'random' strings** – people favour certain letters and positions; real randomness comes from a generator.
131	How the generator works
132	When you click *Regenerate*, the page asks the browser's Web Crypto API for random 32-bit numbers. To pick a character from a set of, say, 70 characters, it discards any number in the small leftover range above the largest multiple of 70 and draws again. This **rejection sampling** removes the 'modulo bias' that simple generators have, where some characters come up slightly more often. One character from each enabled set is placed first, the rest are filled from the combined set, and the whole string is shuffled with a Fisher–Yates shuffle using the same unbiased random source.
133	Passphrases pick words the same way from the EFF long wordlist, which was designed by the Electronic Frontier Foundation for dice-based passphrases: 7,776 common, distinct, easy-to-spell English words, so each word gives exactly log₂(7776) ≈ 12.9 bits. The list is loaded from this site only when you choose passphrase mode.
134	You can check this yourself: open the browser's developer tools, look at the network tab and generate passwords – no requests are made. Or disconnect from the internet and keep generating.
135	Website password rules
136	Many sites demand at least one capital, one digit and one symbol, and some reject certain symbols or limit length. With all four sets enabled, every generated password contains at least one character from each set. If a site refuses a symbol, type it in *Exclude characters*. If a site limits length to, for example, 12 or 20 characters, set the length accordingly – then keep at least 12 characters for good strength. Current guidance from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST SP 800-63B) recommends that sites allow long passwords and passphrases, check new passwords against lists of breached passwords, and avoid forcing arbitrary composition rules and periodic changes – but many sites still have them, so the generator supports both styles.
137	Storing passwords safely
138	Use a **password manager** – Bitwarden, 1Password, KeePassXC, the one built into your browser, or your phone's keychain. You then need to remember only one strong passphrase.
139	Turn on **two-factor authentication** (an authenticator app or a security key) for e-mail, banking, social media and your password manager.
140	Do not store passwords in plain text files, e-mail drafts or chat messages. If you download a list from this page, move the passwords into a manager and delete the file.
141	Write your master passphrase on paper and keep it somewhere safe at home if you fear forgetting it – paper cannot be hacked remotely.
142	Change a password immediately if a site tells you it has been breached, and anywhere you used the same one.
143	PINs and codes
144	A 4-digit PIN has only 10,000 possibilities and a 6-digit PIN a million. That is acceptable only because phones and bank cards lock or wipe after a few wrong tries. Avoid 1234, 0000, years of birth and repeated digits, which together make up a large share of real-world PINs. The PIN mode picks every digit uniformly at random. For anything that can be attacked offline – an encrypted file or a backup – use a long password or passphrase instead.
