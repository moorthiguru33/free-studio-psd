47	How OCR works
48	OCR turns pixels into characters in several stages. First the image is converted to black and white with adaptive thresholding, which copes with uneven lighting and coloured backgrounds. Then the page layout is analysed: the engine finds blocks of text, separates columns, and splits blocks into lines and words. Finally each line is fed to a neural network – an LSTM model trained on hundreds of thousands of lines of text in the chosen language – which predicts the most likely sequence of characters, using its knowledge of the language to choose between look-alikes like 'rn' and 'm' or '0' and 'O'.
49	{brand} uses Tesseract, the open-source OCR engine originally developed at HP and later maintained with Google's support, compiled to WebAssembly so it runs in the browser at near-native speed. The same engine powers many desktop and server OCR products.
50	Getting the most accurate results
51	**Use a sharp, well-lit image.** Blur and shadows are the main causes of errors. Photograph documents flat, in daylight, without the flash glare.
52	**Fill the frame with the text.** Crop away tables, hands and backgrounds with [Crop Image](/crop-image/).
53	**Choose the right language.** A French text read with the English model loses accents and misreads words.
54	**Increase DPI for small print.** If the text is small in the image, raise the resolution setting so letters are at least 20–30 pixels tall.
55	**Straighten the image.** Slight tilts are handled, but strongly rotated photos should be rotated first with [Rotate Image](/rotate-image/).
56	**Higher contrast helps.** Dark text on a light background gives the best results; light text on dark backgrounds usually works too.

77	Languages and scripts
78	Each language has its own trained model, which knows the alphabet and common words. Latin-script languages (English, Spanish, French, German, Portuguese, Italian) share most letters but differ in accents and vocabulary, so choosing the right one improves accuracy noticeably. Russian uses Cyrillic. Arabic is written right to left with letters that change shape depending on their position; the Arabic model handles this and outputs text in the correct logical order. Hindi uses Devanagari with its characteristic headline and conjunct letters, and Tamil has its own script with vowel signs around consonants – both have dedicated models. Chinese (Simplified) and Japanese have thousands of characters and larger models, so their first load takes a little longer.
79	For documents mixing two languages, choose both: every word is then matched against both models, which avoids, for example, English words being mangled in a Hindi document.
80	Text or searchable PDF?
81	Plain text is the most flexible: paste it into a document, e-mail, spreadsheet or translator. A searchable PDF keeps the image exactly as it looks and places the recognised text invisibly on top, so you can search it with Ctrl+F, select and copy passages, and let document management systems index it. Choose PDF when the original layout matters – a signed letter, an ID or a form – and text when you want to reuse the words. For multi-page scanned PDFs, use [OCR PDF](/ocr-pdf/), which adds a text layer to every page and can straighten crooked scans.

114	As with any OCR tool, always proof-read important details such as amounts, account numbers, dates and names. Even a 99% accurate result contains one wrong character in every hundred, and the engine cannot know that a misread digit in an IBAN matters more than a misread letter in a word.
115	Screenshots: the easiest case
116	Screenshots are ideal for OCR because the text is perfectly sharp, straight and evenly lit. To grab text from a video, a locked PDF, an app that does not allow copying, or an image in a chat, take a screenshot (Windows: Win+Shift+S; Mac: Cmd+Shift+4; Android and iPhone: the usual button combination), then open this page and press Ctrl+V. The screenshot is read straight from the clipboard; you never need to save it as a file. On high-resolution displays, screenshots already have enough pixels per letter, so the default DPI is fine.
117	Extracting text from social media and memes
118	Images shared on social networks are usually compressed and may have text over busy backgrounds or coloured gradients. OCR still works well on large captions and on screenshots of posts, tweets and comments. For meme-style white text with black outlines, the engine reads the letters as long as they are big and clear; crop away the picture underneath to reduce noise. Very small text in compressed images – such as a watermark or a username in the corner – may need a higher DPI setting or a cleaner source image. If a post is still online, taking a fresh full-resolution screenshot gives better results than re-saving an already compressed copy several times.
119	Photos taken with a phone
120	Phone photos of documents vary much more than scans. For best results hold the phone parallel to the page so the text is not distorted in perspective, make sure the whole page is in focus by tapping on the text before shooting, and avoid your own shadow by standing to the side of the light source. Turn off the flash for glossy paper, which reflects light and creates white patches with no text. If you shoot in HEIC on an iPhone, you can upload the HEIC directly; it is decoded in the browser. A modern phone camera produces 12 megapixels or more, which is plenty – there is no need to raise the DPI setting for photos.
121	Receipts and invoices
122	Receipts are often printed on thermal paper, which fades over time and has low contrast. Photograph them soon after purchase, on a dark background so the edges are clear, and flatten curled paper. The OCR output will give you the shop name, items and totals as text you can paste into an expense spreadsheet. Because amounts are critical, check the totals by eye before submitting a claim.
123	OCR versus copying text from a PDF
124	If a PDF was created from a word processor, it already contains real text, and you can simply select and copy it or use [PDF to Text](/pdf-to-text/) – faster and 100% accurate. OCR is only needed when the PDF is a scan or a picture of text. A quick test: try to select a word in your PDF viewer. If you can highlight individual words, the text is already there; if the whole page highlights as one image, you need OCR.
125	Glossary
126	**OCR (optical character recognition):** converting images of text into machine-readable characters.
127	**DPI (dots per inch):** how many pixels are used per inch of the original; higher values make small letters larger for the engine.
128	**Text layer:** invisible text placed over an image in a PDF so it can be searched and copied.
129	**LSTM:** the type of neural network used by modern Tesseract to recognise whole lines of text.
130	**Binarisation:** converting the image to pure black and white before recognition.
