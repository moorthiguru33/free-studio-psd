72	How Lanczos resampling works
73	Every pixel in the enlarged image lies between pixels of the original. The simplest method, nearest-neighbour, copies the closest original pixel – fast but blocky. Bilinear interpolation averages the four nearest pixels – smooth but blurry. Bicubic uses sixteen pixels and gives better results. Lanczos goes further: for each new pixel it looks at six original pixels in each direction and weights them by a windowed sinc function, the mathematically ideal reconstruction filter for band-limited signals. In practice this preserves edges and fine textures much better than bilinear or bicubic, while keeping halos small.
74	Because large upscales with one big step can amplify problems, {brand} applies the filter separately horizontally and vertically, which is both faster and exact for this kind of filter. The optional unsharp mask then increases local contrast at edges, which our eyes perceive as sharpness.
75	Denoise before you upscale
76	Images saved as JPG – especially those downloaded from social networks or forwarded through messaging apps – contain compression artefacts: faint 8×8 pixel blocks, ringing around text and colour smudges. Upscaling makes these four or sixteen times bigger and much more visible. The Denoise option applies a gentle blur before enlarging, which smooths the artefacts away; the sharpening step afterwards then restores edges. Use Light for normal web images and Strong for very compressed ones. For clean PNG graphics and photos straight from a camera, leave denoise off.

120	Upscaling different kinds of images
121	**Portraits and faces.** Use 2× or 3×, Denoise Light and Sharpen Medium. Strong sharpening on skin exaggerates pores and noise. Because nothing is invented, the person looks exactly like themselves – a common complaint about AI upscalers is that they subtly change facial features.
122	**Text, documents and screenshots.** Use 2×, Denoise Off and Sharpen Strong, and save as PNG. Letters become smooth and readable, ideal before printing or pasting into a report. If you want to copy the text, use [Image to Text](/image-to-text/) – upscaling small screenshots first can also improve OCR accuracy.
123	**Logos and icons.** Use 4×, Sharpen Strong, PNG output so transparency is kept. For truly scalable logos, a vector (SVG) version is always better than any upscale; ask the designer or brand owner for it.
124	**Product photos.** Use 2–3× with Light denoise and Medium sharpening. Combine with [Remove Background](/remove-background/) for a clean white background required by many marketplaces.
125	**Anime, cartoons and illustrations.** Flat colours and clean lines upscale very well with Lanczos. Use Strong sharpening and PNG.
126	**Old scanned photos.** Scan at the highest resolution your scanner supports first – that captures far more real detail than any upscaler. Then use Light or Strong denoise to reduce grain before enlarging.
127	How upscaling affects file size
128	Doubling width and height multiplies the number of pixels by four; 4× multiplies it by sixteen and 8× by sixty-four. A 1 MB JPG upscaled 4× can become 8–15 MB, and as a PNG it may exceed 50 MB. If you need a smaller file, choose JPG or WebP output, or run the result through [Compress Image](/compress-image/) with a target size. For web use, upscaling beyond the size at which the image is displayed only makes pages slower – resize to the display size instead.
129	Pixels, DPI and PPI explained
130	An image file only has pixels – for example 2400×1600. DPI or PPI (dots or pixels per inch) is a separate number that tells printers how large to print it. Changing DPI from 72 to 300 without changing pixels does not improve quality; it only makes the print smaller. To print larger at the same quality, you need more pixels, which is what upscaling gives you. A useful rule: pixels needed = print size in inches × 300 for close viewing, × 150 for posters. An A4 page (8.3×11.7 in) at 300 PPI needs about 2480×3508 pixels.

172	Common myths about upscaling
173	**'Upscaling recovers the original detail.'** Only the original camera or file has that detail; resampling reconstructs smooth transitions, and AI guesses texture.
174	**'Changing DPI to 300 makes it high resolution.'** DPI is only print metadata – pixels are what count.
175	**'Bigger is always better.'** An image larger than where it will be shown wastes storage and bandwidth.
176	**'You need a powerful server.'** Modern browsers compute even 64-megapixel results on an ordinary laptop in seconds.

179	Upscaling and transparency
180	PNG and WebP images with transparent backgrounds – logos, stickers, cut-out product shots – keep their transparency when upscaled, as long as you choose PNG or WebP output. The resampling filter processes the alpha channel together with colour, so edges stay smooth instead of developing a jagged or coloured fringe. Choosing JPG output fills transparent areas with white, which is useful when a marketplace requires a white background but not when you want to place the image over other content. If your cut-out comes from [Remove Background](/remove-background/), upscale it as PNG and then place it on any background you like. For best results with very fine edges like hair, upscale first and then remove the background, so the cut-out works on more pixels.
181	Glossary
182	**Upscaling:** increasing an image's pixel dimensions.
183	**Resampling:** computing new pixel values from the original ones when changing size.
184	**Lanczos filter:** a high-quality resampling filter based on the sinc function.
185	**Unsharp mask:** a sharpening technique that boosts the difference between the image and a blurred copy.
186	**Artefacts:** unwanted patterns such as JPEG blocks, halos or noise.
187	**Megapixel:** one million pixels; 8000×8000 is 64 megapixels.
