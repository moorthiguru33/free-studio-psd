## Percent vs percentage points
If an interest rate rises from 4% to 5%, it has gone up by 1 percentage point – but by 25% in relative terms. News reports and financial statements mix these up surprisingly often. The calculator shows both, so you can quote figures correctly in reports, presentations and articles.
## Mental shortcuts
10% of any number moves the decimal point one place left; 5% is half of that; 1% moves it two places. So 15% of 80 is 8 + 4 = 12. Also, X% of Y always equals Y% of X: 8% of 50 is the same as 50% of 8, which is 4. These tricks help you check that the calculator's answer is in the right ballpark.

## How age is calculated
The calculator counts full years first, then full months, then remaining days, borrowing days from the previous month when needed – the same method used on official forms. For example, from 31 January to 1 March is 1 month and 1 day in a non-leap year. Totals such as days and weeks are exact counts of calendar days between the two dates.
## Age conventions around the world
Most countries use the international age system shown here, where you are 0 at birth and gain a year on each birthday. South Korea officially switched to this system in 2023, replacing its traditional counting; parts of East Asia still use traditional reckoning informally. When forms ask for age, use the international system unless told otherwise.

## The formula
BMI equals weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. For imperial units, the calculator converts pounds to kilograms (× 0.4536) and inches to centimetres (× 2.54) first. For example, 70 kg and 1.75 m gives 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 22.9. BMI Prime is your BMI divided by the upper limit of the normal range, so values above 1.0 indicate overweight.
## Asian cut-off points
A WHO expert consultation found that people of Asian descent often have higher health risks at lower BMI values. Many countries in Asia therefore use 23 as the threshold for overweight and 27.5 for higher risk. Tick the option to apply these cut-offs. The calculator gives information only and is not medical advice; your data never leaves your device.

## The amortisation formula
The regular payment is P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where P is the amount borrowed, r is the interest rate per period and n is the number of payments. Early payments are mostly interest because the balance is high; over time, more of each payment goes towards the principal. The year-by-year table makes this shift easy to see.
## Mortgages in different countries
Loan structures vary. Thirty-year fixed mortgages are common in the United States, while the UK, Canada and Australia typically fix the rate for 2–5 years within a 25–30-year term, and many European and Asian loans use variable rates. Use the current rate for the whole term to estimate payments, then rerun the numbers with a higher rate to test whether you could afford a rise.

## Stacked discounts explained
When a store offers an extra discount on sale items, each discount applies to the result of the previous one. 40% off followed by an extra 20% off means you pay 60% × 80% = 48% of the original price, a 52% discount rather than 60%. The calculator shows this effective discount so there are no surprises at the till.
## Tax added or included
Prices in North America usually exclude sales tax, which varies by state, province or city. Most other countries show prices including VAT or GST, often 5–27%. Choose “already included” to see how much of the price is tax – useful for business expense claims and VAT refunds for tourists.
