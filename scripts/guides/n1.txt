## Reading and speaking time explained
Reading time uses an average silent reading speed of about 238 words per minute, the figure found in a large 2019 review of reading studies for adult readers of English non-fiction. Speaking time uses about 140 words per minute, a typical pace for presentations and podcasts. Both speeds can be changed in the options, so you can match a slower conference talk, a fast-paced video script or readers working in a second language.
## Word limits around the world
Academic systems treat limits differently. UK universities often allow 10% over or under the stated count, many US college application essays have hard caps (the Common App personal statement allows 650 words), and journals in Europe and Asia usually count abstracts separately. Footnotes, references and headings may or may not be included, so always check the guidance you were given. Paste only the parts that count to see exactly where you stand.
## Keyword density for writers and SEO
The keyword list ignores common short words such as “the” and “and” and shows how often each meaningful word appears as a percentage of the total. Search engines reward natural writing, so if one keyword makes up more than 2–3% of a short article, try synonyms or rephrasing. The list is also useful for editors who want to catch repetition.

## Characters, bytes and graphemes
Computers can count text in different ways. A grapheme is what a reader sees as one character, such as “é” or “👍🏽”. Under the hood that may be several code points, and in UTF-8 each code point takes one to four bytes. Latin letters use one byte, most European accented letters two, and Chinese, Japanese, Hindi or Arabic characters usually three. This is why a 255-byte database field may hold far fewer than 255 characters of non-English text.
## Platform limits at a glance
The limit bars cover common targets: 280 characters for a standard X post, 2,200 for an Instagram caption, 3,000 for a LinkedIn post, 100 for a YouTube title and 125 characters before Facebook shows “See more”. Google does not use a fixed character limit for titles and snippets; it truncates by pixel width, so 50–60 and 150–160 characters are safe guides rather than rules.

## Case styles for writers
Style guides differ: American publications often use Title Case for headlines, while many British and European newspapers and most UX writing guides prefer Sentence case. Whatever you choose, apply it consistently across headings, navigation and buttons. Title Case here keeps small words like “of” and “the” lowercase in the middle of a title, which matches the most common English headline rules.
## Case styles for developers
Programming communities have strong conventions: camelCase for JavaScript and Java variables, PascalCase for classes and React components, snake_case for Python, Ruby and SQL, CONSTANT_CASE for constants and environment variables, and kebab-case for URLs, CSS and command-line flags. The converter splits words at spaces, punctuation and existing case changes, so “userProfileID”, “user_profile_id” and “User Profile ID” all convert cleanly.

## How the comparison works
The tool uses a longest-common-subsequence algorithm, the same family of methods behind Git and the Unix diff command. It first finds the lines the two texts share, then marks everything else as added or removed. When a removed line is directly followed by an added one, it treats them as a changed line and runs a second, word-level comparison so you can see the exact edit – a changed date, a new clause, or a corrected spelling.
## Privacy for sensitive comparisons
Many online diff tools send both texts to a server. Here the comparison runs entirely in your browser, which matters for legal drafts, HR documents, unreleased code and personal data covered by regulations such as GDPR. You can even disconnect from the internet after the page loads and the tool will keep working.
