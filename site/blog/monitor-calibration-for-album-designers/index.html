Monitor Calibration for Album Designers

If your albums print darker, warmer or more saturated than they looked on screen, the monitor is usually the cause. Calibration brings your screen close to a known standard so what you see is closer to what the lab prints. This guide explains why calibration matters, how to do it and what to do if you cannot buy a calibrator yet.

By Guru Moorthi · Updated 2026-10-24 · 4 min read

Why screens and prints disagree

Most monitors ship far too bright, often 300 to 400 nits, with a cool blue white point. Prints are viewed by reflected light and cannot be as bright. When you edit on a bright screen, you unconsciously darken photos, and the print comes back muddy. Colour shifts add to the problem: a blue-tinted screen makes you warm up photos too much.

What calibration does

A hardware calibrator, a small sensor placed on the screen, measures the colours your monitor actually shows and creates an ICC profile that corrects them. Your operating system and Photoshop use this profile to display colours more accurately.

Target settings for print work

  • Brightness: 100 to 120 cd/m² (nits). This is the single most important setting.
  • White point: D65 (6500K) is the common standard. Some print workflows prefer D50, but D65 is fine for most album work.
  • Gamma: 2.2.

Set these targets in the calibration software and follow its steps. The process takes ten to fifteen minutes.

Calibrating without a device

Without a calibrator, you can still improve accuracy:

Reduce brightness

Lower the monitor brightness until a white page on screen looks similar to a sheet of white paper held next to it under your normal room light.

Use built-in presets

Many monitors have an sRGB mode. It often limits brightness and colour to a more neutral setting.

Use operating system tools

Windows has Display Colour Calibration and macOS has a display calibrator assistant. They help with gamma and white balance by eye, though less precisely than hardware.

Choosing a monitor

For album work, an IPS panel with close to 100 percent sRGB coverage is a sensible minimum. Wide-gamut monitors show more colours but can make sRGB files look oversaturated if colour management is not set up correctly. Matte screens reduce reflections in bright studios.

Room lighting matters

Edit in consistent, moderate lighting. Avoid strong coloured light falling on the screen, and do not sit with a bright window behind the monitor. Check prints under daylight or a neutral LED, not a yellow tube light.

Test prints close the loop

Even a calibrated screen does not guarantee a perfect match, because paper and lab settings vary. Print one test spread with skin tones, a dark area and a bright area, then compare it with your screen. If the print is darker, lower the monitor brightness slightly rather than brightening every photo. Our RGB vs CMYK guide covers related colour settings.

How often to calibrate

Monitors drift over time. Recalibrate every four to six weeks for professional work, and after any change in monitor settings.

Put it into practice

Colour settings in Photoshop

Calibration works best when Photoshop's colour settings are consistent. Go to Edit > Color Settings and choose a preset such as North America General Purpose or Europe General Purpose, which uses sRGB as the RGB working space. Tick the options to ask when opening files with mismatched profiles, so you know when a template uses Adobe RGB. Convert those files to sRGB before export if your lab prints sRGB.

Soft proofing

If your lab provides an ICC profile for its printer and paper, install it and use View > Proof Setup > Custom to simulate the print on screen. Soft proofing helps you spot colours that will shift, such as intense saree reds or saturated stage lights. Many Indian labs do not share profiles, in which case a test print remains the most reliable check.

Laptops and second screens

Laptop screens vary widely and many cover only part of sRGB. If you edit on a laptop, connect a calibrated external monitor for colour decisions and use the laptop screen only for panels and tools. Avoid judging colour on phones; their brightness and colour settings change automatically.

Signs your monitor needs attention

  • Prints always darker: screen brightness too high.
  • Prints warmer or yellower: screen white point too cool or blue.
  • Shadows blocked up in print: gamma or black level set incorrectly.
  • Different results on two monitors: at least one is not calibrated.

With a calibrated screen, follow our colour grading guide and export settings for print. Test on a sheet from the 12x36 wedding album category, a studio background portrait and a light album background to check how gradients and skin print.

Frequently asked questions

Why do my album prints look darker than my screen?

Your monitor is probably too bright. Calibrate to about 100 to 120 nits so you edit at a print-friendly brightness.

Can I calibrate a monitor without a device?

You can improve it by lowering brightness and using system calibration tools, but a hardware calibrator is far more accurate.

How often should I calibrate my monitor?

Every four to six weeks for professional work, or whenever you change display settings.

What brightness should a monitor be for print editing?

Around 100 to 120 cd/m², much lower than most monitors' default brightness.

Do I need a wide-gamut monitor for album design?

No. A good sRGB IPS monitor is enough for most album work, as labs typically print from sRGB files.
