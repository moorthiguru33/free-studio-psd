Managing Album Design Revisions with Clients

Endless revisions are the biggest drain on an album designer's time. A clear process for proofs and feedback keeps clients happy, protects your schedule and gets albums to the lab faster. This guide covers practical steps for managing album revisions professionally.

By Guru Moorthi · Updated 2026-10-23 · 4 min read

Set expectations before design

Agree on the essentials at the start: album size, number of sheets, number of revision rounds, and the timeline. Two rounds of changes are common; extra rounds can carry a fee. Put this in writing, even in a simple message, so both sides remember the agreement.

Get photo selection approved first

Most revisions come from photo changes, not design. Ask the couple to approve or shortlist photos before you start layouts. Shared online galleries with favourites make this easy. Our guide on organising photos before album design explains how to prepare.

Ask for must-have lists

Request a list of must-include people and moments from the couple and both sets of parents. It prevents the late discovery that an important uncle is missing.

Send clear proofs

Use a PDF or low-resolution images

Combine sheets into a single PDF with page numbers, or send numbered JPGs at reduced size. Never send full-resolution print files as proofs. Our guide to making a PDF for client approval shows how.

Watermark proofs

A subtle watermark discourages printing proofs elsewhere. See how to add a studio logo or watermark.

Collect feedback in one place

Ask the client to send all feedback in one message per round, listing the sheet number and the change, for example: Sheet 07, replace photo 3 with a group photo. Scattered voice notes and calls lead to missed changes.

Use a simple change log

Keep a list of every requested change and tick it off as you make it. Send the list back with the next proof so the client can see everything was handled.

Making changes efficiently

Organised PSDs make revisions fast. Keep photos as Smart Objects so you can use Replace Contents, and keep layers named. See Smart Objects for album design and how to speed up your album design workflow.

Handling difficult requests

  • Too many photos per sheet: explain that crowded pages make faces small, and offer an extra sheet instead.
  • Changing the whole style late: this is a redesign, not a revision; discuss timeline and cost.
  • Conflicting feedback from family members: ask the couple to confirm a single final list.

Stay polite and refer back to the agreement. Most clients appreciate a clear explanation.

Final approval

Before sending to the lab, ask for written final approval of the last proof, including names, dates and spellings. Once approved, export print files following the print-ready PSD checklist and send files to the lab.

Templates that make revisions easier

A sample proof message

A clear proof message saves back-and-forth. For example: Here is the first draft of your album, 30 sheets plus cover. Please review each page and send all changes in one message by Saturday, listing the sheet number and the change. This draft includes two revision rounds as agreed. After your second round of changes we will send the final proof for approval.

Timelines that work

Give clients a clear deadline for each round, usually five to seven days. Without a deadline, feedback can take months and albums sit unfinished. If a client misses the deadline, send a polite reminder and explain that the album will move back in your queue.

Tools that help

  • Shared online galleries for photo selection with favourites and comments.
  • Numbered PDF proofs so feedback references sheet numbers.
  • A simple spreadsheet to track each client's revision round and status.
  • Template naming conventions so you can find and change any sheet quickly.

Learning from revisions

After each album, note what clients changed most often. If many ask for more family photos, increase family collages in your first draft. If clients often change backgrounds, show two background options at the start. Over time, first drafts get closer to final and revisions shrink.

Remember that clear communication is part of the product. Clients who understand the process feel more confident, give better feedback and are more likely to recommend you to friends planning their own weddings. A short, friendly explanation of how proofs and revisions work, sent at the start of every project, sets the tone for the whole album.

Templates with clearly named layers and separate photo frames are faster to revise. Try sheets from the 12x36 wedding album category, the 12x30 album category and the album background category. For more on running an album service, read how to start an album design service from home.

Frequently asked questions

How many revisions should an album design include?

Two rounds of changes is common. Agree the number before starting and charge for extra rounds if needed.

How should I send album proofs to clients?

Send a numbered PDF or low-resolution watermarked JPGs, never full print files.

How do I reduce album revisions?

Get the photo selection approved first and collect feedback in one consolidated list per round.

How long should clients get to review an album proof?

Five to seven days per round is common and keeps projects moving.

What if a client keeps asking for changes after approval?

Refer to the agreed revision rounds and explain that further changes after approval may carry a fee and delay printing.

Should I charge for extra revision rounds?

Many studios do, at a fixed fee per round, once the agreed rounds are used. State this clearly in your initial agreement.
